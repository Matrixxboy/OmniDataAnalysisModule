Sample vs Population
The foundation of all statistical inference

Before calculating anything in statistics, the first question is always: are you working with everyone, or just some of them? That distinction changes your formulas, your conclusions, and your confidence.

Population (N)
The entire group
Every single member of the group you care about — all students in a country, all products in a factory run, all voters in a nation. Complete and exhaustive.
Sample (n)
A selected subset
A carefully chosen portion of the population used to make inferences about the whole group. Must be representative to be valid.
Visual — Population vs Sample
Conceptual chart: Population = all 100 members, Sample = 20 selected members
Key idea: A population gives you exact, true values (called parameters). A sample gives you estimates (called statistics) that approximate the population parameters.
Notation Reference
Population size → N (capital) Sample size → n (lowercase) Population mean → μ (mu) Sample mean → x̄ (x-bar) Population std dev → σ (sigma) Sample std dev → s (s)
Rule of thumb: Greek letters = population parameters. Latin letters = sample statistics.

A well-drawn sample should represent the population accurately. Random sampling is the gold standard — it ensures every member has an equal chance of being selected, removing selection bias.

How to collect a good sample:
1
Define your population clearly — who exactly are you trying to understand?
2
Choose a sampling method — random, stratified, cluster, or systematic.
3
Determine sample size — larger samples reduce error but cost more.
4
Check for bias — make sure no group is systematically excluded.

When to use each:
Use Population when...
• The group is small enough to measure fully
• Data already exists for everyone (census, records)
• Absolute precision is required (e.g. legal compliance)
• You are studying a closed system (all employees at one company)
Use Sample when...
• The population is too large or inaccessible
• Time or budget is limited
• Measurement is destructive (e.g. testing product lifespan)
• You are doing surveys, clinical trials, or market research
Real example: A country of 1.4 billion people wants to know average household income. Surveying every household is impossible — so they survey 100,000 households (sample) and use those results to estimate the national average (population parameter).
Advantages of Sampling
• Fast and cost-effective
• Feasible when population is huge
• Enables repeated studies
• Less data management burden
Limitations of Sampling
• Always has some error
• Biased if not randomly drawn
• May miss rare subgroups
• Results are estimates, not truth

Variance & Standard Deviation
How spread out is your data from the mean?

Two datasets can have the same average but look completely different. Variance and standard deviation measure how far individual values are from the mean — quantifying the spread or dispersion of the data.

Chart — Low vs High Spread (same mean, different variance)
Two curves with mean=50: one narrow (low variance), one wide (high variance)
Variance (σ² or s²)
Average squared deviation
Measures how far each data point is from the mean, squared and averaged. Squaring removes negatives and amplifies large deviations.
Standard Deviation (σ or s)
Square root of variance
Variance brought back to the original unit of measurement. Much easier to interpret — "spread in the same unit as the data."
Population Variance & Standard Deviation
Population Variance: σ² = Σ(xᵢ − μ)² / N Population Standard Deviation: σ = √[ Σ(xᵢ − μ)² / N ]
xᵢ = each individual value  |  μ = population mean  |  N = total population size  |  Σ = sum of all values
Sample Variance & Standard Deviation (Bessel's Correction)
Sample Variance: s² = Σ(xᵢ − x̄)² / (n − 1) Sample Standard Deviation: s = √[ Σ(xᵢ − x̄)² / (n − 1) ]
x̄ = sample mean  |  n = sample size  |  (n − 1) = Bessel's correction — dividing by n would underestimate the true population variance, so we use n−1 to correct for this bias.
Why (n − 1) and not n? When you compute the sample mean x̄, the data "uses up" one degree of freedom. The sample mean is always exactly at the center of the sample, which means we're not fully free — we've constrained the system. Dividing by (n − 1) corrects for this, giving an unbiased estimate of the population variance.
Step-by-step example — computing variance for [4, 8, 6, 10, 2]:
1
Find mean: x̄ = (4+8+6+10+2) / 5 = 6
2
Subtract mean from each value: (4−6)=−2, (8−6)=+2, (6−6)=0, (10−6)=+4, (2−6)=−4
3
Square each deviation: 4, 4, 0, 16, 16
4
Sum the squares: 4+4+0+16+16 = 40
5
Sample variance: s² = 40 / (5−1) = 10
6
Sample std deviation: s = √10 ≈ 3.16 (in the original units)

Skewness — Shape Direction
Is your data leaning left, right, or perfectly balanced?

Skewness tells you whether your distribution has a long tail on one side. Even when the mean and standard deviation are similar, two datasets can have very different shapes — and skewness captures that asymmetry.

Chart — Three types of skewness compared
Left-skewed (negative), Symmetric (zero skew), Right-skewed (positive)
Negative Skew
Left-Skewed
Long tail on the left. Most data on the right (high values). Mean < Median.
Zero / Symmetric
Normal / Bell Curve
Perfectly balanced. Mean = Median = Mode. No tail bias.
Positive Skew
Right-Skewed
Long tail on the right. Most data on the left (low values). Mean > Median.
Pearson's Skewness Formula (simplified)
Skewness = 3 × (Mean − Median) / Standard Deviation Or using moments (more precise): Skewness = [ n / ((n−1)(n−2)) ] × Σ[ (xᵢ − x̄) / s ]³
Result < 0 → Left-skewed (negative skew)
Result = 0 → Symmetric (no skew)
Result > 0 → Right-skewed (positive skew)

Rule of thumb: |skewness| < 0.5 = roughly symmetric  |  0.5–1.0 = moderately skewed  |  > 1.0 = highly skewed

Memory trick: The skew is named after where the tail points — not where the bulk of data is. A right-skewed distribution has a long right tail (a few very high values), even though most of the data sits on the left.

Kurtosis — Peak & Tail Shape
How extreme are the extremes? How sharp is the peak?

While skewness measures direction, kurtosis measures intensity — specifically how sharp the peak is and how heavy the tails are. High kurtosis means more outliers are lurking in the tails than you'd expect from a normal distribution.

Chart — Three kurtosis types compared to normal distribution
Leptokurtic (tall/narrow), Mesokurtic (normal), Platykurtic (flat/wide)
Leptokurtic
Tall Peak, Heavy Tails
Sharp, high peak. Thick tails with more extreme outliers than normal. "Tall and thin." Excess kurtosis > 0.
Mesokurtic
Normal — The Baseline
Normal distribution shape. Moderate peak, moderate tails. Excess kurtosis = 0. This is the reference point.
Platykurtic
Flat Peak, Thin Tails
Broad, flat peak. Light tails with fewer extreme outliers. "Short and fat." Excess kurtosis < 0.
Kurtosis Formula (fourth moment)
Kurtosis (κ) = [ Σ(xᵢ − x̄)⁴ / n ] / s⁴ Excess Kurtosis (used in most software): Excess Kurtosis = κ − 3 Sample formula (unbiased): = [ n(n+1) / ((n−1)(n−2)(n−3)) ] × Σ[(xᵢ−x̄)/s]⁴ − 3(n−1)² / ((n−2)(n−3))
Normal distribution kurtosis = 3 (raw)  |  Excess kurtosis = 0
Leptokurtic: excess kurtosis > 0  |  Platykurtic: excess kurtosis < 0

Most software (Excel, R, Python scipy.stats) returns excess kurtosis — already minus 3.
Why subtract 3? A perfect normal distribution has kurtosis = 3. Subtracting 3 gives "excess kurtosis," which makes interpretation easier: positive means more extreme than normal, negative means less extreme. This is why it's also called excess kurtosis.

• Quality control: identifying rare but extreme defects
• Insurance: modeling rare catastrophic claims

• Critical for financial and actuarial models
• Flags non-normality in data

Outliers
The rebels of your dataset

An outlier is a data point that differs significantly from other observations. They can be caused by measurement or execution errors, or they might indicate a genuine, rare anomaly in the underlying process.

A group of anomalous data points. Indicates a potential structural shift or a new subgroup forming.

A Box and Whisker plot visually summarizes a dataset's distribution. The box represents the Interquartile Range (IQR) containing the middle 50% of the data. The whiskers extend to the lowest and highest values still within a 1.5× IQR safety buffer. Any points falling outside these whiskers are mathematically considered outliers.

Method 1: Z-Score (Best for Normally Distributed Data)
Z = (X − μ) / σ
X = Specific Data Point   |   μ = Population Mean   |   σ = Standard Deviation
How it works:
1
Find the Mean (μ) and Std Dev (σ) of your entire dataset.
2
Subtract the mean from a specific data point (X). This tells you its absolute distance from the center.
3
Divide by the Standard Deviation (σ). This converts the distance into "standard units." A Z-score of 2 means the point is exactly 2 standard deviations away from the mean.
4
Flag Outliers: Any data point with a Z-score greater than +3 or less than -3 is typically flagged as an outlier (it falls outside 99.7% of expected normal data).
Limitation: Because Z-Score relies on the Mean and Std Dev (which are themselves easily skewed by outliers), this method struggles if you have massive, extreme anomalies already polluting the dataset.
Method 2: IQR Boxplot (Best for Skewed or Messy Data)
IQR = Q3 − Q1 Lower Bound = Q1 − (1.5 × IQR) Upper Bound = Q3 + (1.5 × IQR)
Q1 = 25th Percentile (lower quartile)   |   Q3 = 75th Percentile (upper quartile)
How it works:
1
Find Quartile 1 (Q1) and Quartile 3 (Q3). Q1 is the median of the lower half of your data. Q3 is the median of the upper half.
2
Calculate the IQR. Subtract Q1 from Q3. This is the "middle box" containing 50% of your data.
3
Multiply IQR by 1.5. This gives you a safe "buffer zone" distance extending outwards.
4
Set the Fences. Subtract the buffer from Q1 (Lower Bound), and add it to Q3 (Upper Bound). Any value falling outside these fences is an outlier.
Advantage: Medians and Quartiles are "robust" to outliers. One massive anomaly won't shift Q1 or Q3 very much, making this a much safer detection method for messy data than the Z-Score.
Why Outliers are Useful (and Dangerous):
The Good (Useful Insights)
• Fraud detection: Unusual credit card transactions.
• Medical discovery: A patient reacting unexpectedly well to a drug.
• System health: Identifying a failing server or sensor.
• Market gaps: Identifying unserved highly-profitable niches.
The Bad (Statistical Problems)
• Skewed averages: One billionaire ruins the average income metric.
• Model disruption: Linear regression is highly sensitive to outliers.
• Variance explosion: They massively inflate variance and std dev.
• False conclusions: Taking action based on noisy, errant data.
How to Handle Them
Keep them: If they represent real, important phenomena.
Remove them (Trimming): If they are definitely errors/typos.
Cap them (Winsorizing): Limit extreme values to a percentile threshold (e.g., 99th).
Transform data: Use log transformations to reduce the pull of extreme values.
Common Mistakes
Blindly deleting outliers without investigating why they occurred.
Using the mean (instead of median) when extreme outliers exist.
Assuming all outliers are "bad data."
